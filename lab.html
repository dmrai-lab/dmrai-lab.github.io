The laboratory · a research object

We study the science.
And the laboratory.

dmrai-lab is one human, Rutger Fick, and a team of AI agents led by Claude. We run two research programmes at once: replayable Monte Carlo for diffusion MRI, and the laboratory that builds it.

Two programmes, one inside the other.

The science

Simulate once, read any acquisition, with its error stated. Certified, composable Monte Carlo for diffusion MRI, and open substrate data for anyone to read.

The laboratory

How a small lab of one human and AI agents compounds: how verified work becomes the instrument for the next piece, how agents are briefed, checked and corrected, and how we measure whether the lab is actually getting better.

How it compounds.

  1. Every result is checked against physics. Exact references (closed forms and exact solvers) are the lab's reviewers. A result that disagrees with them does not merge.
  2. Every checked result becomes an instrument. A closed form written to validate one feature becomes the test for the next. A reference built for one physical effect becomes the yardstick for another.
  3. Every failure becomes a guard. When something slips through, we fix its cause and add the check that would have caught it, so the same mistake costs us once.
  4. Every week, the lab looks at itself. Agents report the friction they met in their own words; their working records show where effort or context was lost; and each week we fix the most recurring problem in the tooling itself.

What it is not.

This is not self-improving AI. The models do not change. The laboratory around them does: its references, its tests, the harnesses the agents work in, and the habits they are briefed with.

A human sets the questions and approves everything that leaves the lab. And compounding only counts when it is verified: speed without checks just produces plausible errors faster.

Measured, not claimed.

We track the laboratory like an experiment: what lands each week, how often work needs another round before it is accepted, how many defects the references catch before anything merges, and how much working context is lost and recovered. The first measurement is public already: the activity strip on our History page. More will follow as the data accumulates, including the weeks that do not flatter us.
